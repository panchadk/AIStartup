1. The big picture

You have now used every tool of an applied AI team: pretrained vision models, embeddings and search, retrieval-augmented chatbots, fine-tuning, careful data and evaluation, and deployment. The capstone asks you to use them together, on a problem you chose.

The hard part is no longer any single technique. It is judgment: what to build, in what order, what to leave out, and how to tell whether it works. This module is practice in that judgment, and the workbook turns each decision into something you can check.

2. Choose and scope a project you can finish

A good capstone has one user, one painful problem, data you can really get, and a number that means "good enough". You wrote this in Module 10. Now decide what is in.

  • Must have (3 to 6 items): without these there is no product. If everything is a must, nothing is.
  • Nice to have: only if time is left, and you decide in advance what you drop first.
  • Out of scope: things you will not do, written down. It is easy to drift into them.

Then check that it fits the calendar. Add up the days of the must-have work, share them among the team, and add a 20% buffer, because plans always run late. If it does not fit, cut scope. Cutting the buffer is the one thing that never works.

per person       = must-have days / team size
needed with buffer = per person x 1.2
fits             = needed with buffer <= days available

3. Build the thin slice first

The natural plan is to finish the data, then the model, then the API, then the page. The trouble is that nothing works end to end until the last week, and that is when you learn that the model's output does not match what the API expects, or that real inputs crash the pipeline.

The better plan is the thinnest end-to-end version: a crude version of every layer that takes a real input and produces a real output. Then improve the weakest layer first. The rule: make it work, then make it good.

  • Test it with real inputs (as users really send them), not examples you made easy.
  • A smoke test sends every real input through the whole pipeline and reports crashes and time. A crash on day 3 is a gift.
  • Write down how to run it and hand the instructions to someone outside your team, without helping. Where they get stuck is a bug in your instructions.

4. Build or buy?

For each part of your product, ask: what is the simplest thing that could work? A pretrained model or a hosted API is the cheapest start. Prompting and retrieval come next (Modules 6 and 7). Fine-tuning (Modules 5 and 8) comes when you need a fixed format, a lower cost per request, or a skill the base model lacks. Training from scratch is rarely right for a startup.

Your evaluation tells you when to move down: if the simple option already meets your target, stop. If it falls short, look at the failures, and choose the next rung for a reason you can name.

5. Working as a team

The usual failure of a team project is not a weak person. It is parts that do not fit when joined. Three habits prevent it:

  1. Agree interfaces first. For every component, write its input type, its output type, and one owner. The output of each part must equal the input of the next. A tiny function can check this.
  2. Split work by component, not by task type, so that each person owns something end to end and can test it alone.
  3. Short daily check-ins and Git branches. Ten minutes a day: what I did, what I will do, what blocks me. Merge small, merge often.

Working alone? You still need the contracts: they are how you keep your own parts from drifting apart.

6. Evaluating honestly

You built an evaluation harness in Module 9. Reuse its principles: a fixed evaluation set that the model has never seen, a fingerprint to prove it did not change, and a baseline beside your model.

  • The baseline is the simple approach a smart person would try first (a keyword rule, the majority class, plain search). If you do not beat it by a clear margin, either the problem is easy or your model is not adding much, and you have learned that cheaply.
  • The target comes from the user's need, before you look at results. Do not move it to match what you achieved.
  • A fair evaluation set looks like real use. If 12% of real tickets are urgent but your evaluation set is half urgent, say so, and know what that does to precision.
  • Read the failures. The score says how much fails; the failures say why. The biggest pattern is your next step.

7. Test with real users early

Your own sense of how usable your product is cannot be trusted: you know too much. Put it in front of people who have never seen it, give them a real task, and watch without helping.

  • Record for each person: did they finish the task, how satisfied were they, and what issues did they hit.
  • Count the issues. The one that happens to most people comes first.
  • Fix what you found, then test again with new people: three small rounds beat one big one.

9. Common pitfalls

  • A scope so big that the plan can never fit.
  • Perfecting one layer while the others do not exist.
  • Interfaces that live only in people's heads.
  • Test inputs that are easier than real ones.
  • Reporting a score with no baseline and no target.
  • Moving the target after seeing the result.
  • Showing the product to no one until the last day.

10. Check yourself

Try each question first, then open the answer.

Question 1: Your must-have work totals 18 days, your team has 3 people, and each person has 7 days. With a 20% buffer, does the plan fit?

18 / 3 = 6 days per person. With the buffer: 6 x 1.2 = 7.2 days, which is more than the 7 available, so it does not fit (by 0.2 days). Cut a small must-have item, or move one to nice-to-have, rather than dropping the buffer.

Question 2: Your model scores 0.71 on your metric, the baseline scores 0.69, and your target is 0.85. What do you conclude, and what do you do?

The model barely beats the baseline (2 points) and misses the target by 14. Do not claim success. First read the failures and check the data and the evaluation set (size, fairness, label quality). Then decide whether more or better data, a different approach, or a more realistic target is called for. Write down the reasoning: an honest 'not yet' with a plan is a good result.

Question 3: Why test with five users rather than fifty?

Most usability problems show up in the first few sessions, and further users of the same kind mostly repeat the same issues (a rule of thumb says five find about 85%). Five is cheap enough to do often. Three small rounds of five, with fixes in between, find more than one round of fifteen.
