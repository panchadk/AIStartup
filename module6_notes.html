1. The big picture

Ordinary search matches words. Ask for "pause the program" and it looks for pages containing "pause", "program" and nothing else. A page that says "sleep: delay execution for a number of seconds" is a perfect answer, but it shares no words with your question, so keyword search misses it.

Semantic search matches meaning. It turns the question and every passage into numbers that capture meaning, and then finds the passages closest to the question. This is the engine inside many AI products: document search, support bots, recommendation, and the "retrieval" step of the chatbots you will build in Module 7.

No training in this module. You use a pretrained embedding model as it is. Your skill is in chunking, ranking, and, above all, measuring whether the search works.

2. Embeddings: meaning as numbers

An embedding is a list of numbers (a vector) that represents a piece of text. The model you use outputs 384 numbers per text. The key property: texts with similar meaning get similar vectors.

It helps to picture each vector as a point on a map. Real maps have 384 dimensions, which we cannot draw, but the idea is the same as in two dimensions:

So searching means finding the points nearest to the question's point. The same model must embed both the documents and the question, otherwise their maps do not match.

3. How a transformer makes an embedding

Embedding models are transformers. You do not need the details yet, but the central idea is attention: when the model reads a word, it looks at all the other words and decides how much each one matters.

  • Text is first cut into tokens: words or pieces of words.
  • Attention lets each token pull in information from the tokens that matter for it. The word "bank" gets a different meaning next to "river" than next to "loan".
  • The model combines everything into one vector for the whole text.
  • Models read a limited length (a few hundred tokens for this one; the notebook prints the exact limit). Text beyond it is simply ignored, which is one reason to chunk.

4. Cosine similarity

To compare the question's vector with a passage's vector, we use the cosine similarity: the cosine of the angle between them.

cosine(a, b) = (a . b) / (length of a x length of b)

A tiny example: a = [1, 0] and b = [1, 1]. The dot product is 1. The lengths are 1 and about 1.414. So the cosine is 1 / 1.414 = 0.707, which is the cosine of 45 degrees.

  • It ignores length. Only direction counts, so a long passage and a short one about the same thing can still match.
  • With unit-length vectors, cosine equals the dot product. That is why we normalize the embeddings: the whole search becomes one matrix multiplication.
  • Use the ranking, not the number. What counts as a "high" score depends on the model. Compare scores within one search, not across models.

5. Chunking your documents

You rarely embed a whole document. A 20-page document has dozens of topics, and one vector cannot represent all of them. So you cut it into chunks and embed each one.

  • Small chunks are focused, so a specific question matches them well. But they can lose the context around them.
  • Large chunks carry context, but the vector blurs several topics together, so matching gets vaguer.
  • Keep paragraphs whole. Cutting a passage in half makes both halves harder to find. Pack whole paragraphs together up to the limit, and cut inside a paragraph only when it is longer than the limit by itself.
  • Remember where each chunk came from (the file name) so you can show sources.
  • Overlap is an option you may meet elsewhere: neighbouring chunks share a few sentences so that nothing falls exactly on a boundary. With paragraph-aware chunking you need it less.

6. The search pipeline

Embedding all your chunks is done once, ahead of time, and the vectors are stored. When a question arrives, you embed it, compare it with every stored vector, and return the top few.

How big can this get? A vector of 384 numbers uses 384 x 4 bytes = 1,536 bytes. For 100,000 chunks that is about 154 MB, which fits in memory, and comparing one question against all of them takes a few milliseconds. This "brute force" approach is perfectly fine up to hundreds of thousands of chunks. For many millions, products use approximate nearest-neighbour indexes (for example FAISS or a vector database) that trade a little accuracy for speed.

7. Measuring a search engine

Trying five queries and feeling good is not evidence. You need a test set: queries written by you, each with a way to recognize a correct result.

  • Write queries the way a user would ask, not by copying words from the document. If you copy words, you only test keyword matching.
  • Gold phrases: for each query, a phrase that marks the right passage (and is not in the query). A retrieved chunk is relevant if it contains one.
  • Hit rate at k (recall at k): the share of queries for which a relevant chunk appears in the top k results. Your target: at least 15 of 20 in the top 3.
  • Beat a baseline. Compare with plain keyword search. If your semantic search is not clearly better, find out why.

A fairness trap when you compare chunk sizes

If every chunk size returns its top 3, a size with 300-word chunks returns about 900 words, while 30-word chunks return about 90. The big ones are more likely to contain the answer by luck. To compare sizes fairly, give each the same text budget. For example, around 360 words: 12 chunks of 30 words, 6 of 60, 3 of 120, and 1 of 300.

Reading the failures

For every missed query, ask: was the answer not in the documents? Was the gold phrase too narrow? Was the chunk cut badly? Or did the model really fail? Only the last one is a model problem, and the other three are about your data and your test.

Making it better

  • Re-ranking: fetch the top 20 quickly, then let a slower, smarter model that reads the question and passage together (a cross-encoder) pick the best 3.
  • Hybrid search: combine keyword matching (good for exact names and codes) with semantic matching (good for paraphrases).

8. Key terms

TermPlain meaning
EmbeddingA vector of numbers that represents the meaning of a text
Semantic searchFinding text by meaning rather than by exact words
Cosine similarityHow closely two vectors point the same way, from -1 to 1
AttentionHow a model weighs which other words matter for each word
ChunkA focused passage cut from a longer document
Top-kThe k best matches for a query
Gold phraseA phrase that marks a correct passage in your test set
Hit rate (recall at k)The share of queries with a relevant result in the top k
Re-rankingA second, slower step that reorders the top results

9. Common pitfalls

  • Embedding the documents with one model and the question with another.
  • Test queries that copy words from the document.
  • Chunks that cut a passage in half, or chunks so big that topics blur.
  • Comparing chunk sizes with the same k instead of the same text budget.
  • Sorting results the wrong way round.
  • Judging the system by a handful of examples.
  • Changing the chunk size and forgetting to embed again.

10. Check yourself

Try each question first, then open the answer.

Question 1: A question has a cosine similarity of 0.82 with passage A and 0.31 with passage B. What can you conclude?

Passage A is much closer in meaning to the question than passage B, so A ranks higher. Do not read too much into the absolute numbers, because what counts as high depends on the model. The ranking is what matters.

Question 2: You embed 100,000 chunks with a 384-number model stored as 32-bit floats. How much memory do the vectors need?

100,000 x 384 x 4 bytes = 153,600,000 bytes, about 154 MB. That fits comfortably in memory on a normal laptop.

Question 3: With k = 3, your 300-word chunks score 17 of 20 and your 60-word chunks score 15 of 20. Can you conclude that larger chunks are better?

No. At k = 3, the 300-word chunks return about 900 words and the 60-word chunks only about 180, so the larger chunks had five times more text in which to find the answer. Compare at the same text budget before concluding anything.
