1. The big picture

In Module 1 you trained one model once and looked at its accuracy. Real work is different. You try many settings, compare them, and need to trust the results.

This module builds the machinery for that: a reusable training function, honest evaluation, ways to stop a model from memorizing, and a record of every experiment. You will write your own fit() function, the same shape of function that libraries such as PyTorch Lightning and Keras provide.

2. Three splits of your data

A model can look excellent on data it has already seen. To know how it will do on new data, you keep some data hidden. You need three piles, not two:

  • Training set: the model learns its weights from this.
  • Validation set: you use it to make decisions: the learning rate, the model size, how much dropout, when to stop.
  • Test set: your final exam. You look at it once.
Why not just two piles? Every time you pick the setting that scores best on a set, you slightly fit your choices to that set. After many choices, its score is too optimistic. If you tune on the test set, you have no clean exam left. That is why the validation set exists.

We use only 2,000 training images on purpose. With so little data, overfitting is easy to see, which makes it a good place to learn.

3. Reading the loss curves

Plot the training loss and validation loss against epochs. Their shapes tell you what is wrong.

What you seeDiagnosisWhat to try
Both losses high and flatUnderfittingBigger model, train longer, higher learning rate
Both low and closeGood fitYou are done, or try small improvements
Training falls, validation risesOverfittingMore data, dropout, weight decay, early stopping

Note: with dropout on, the training metrics are measured while units are switched off, so training accuracy looks a little lower than it really is. Always judge the model by the validation numbers.

  • Checkpoint: a saved copy of the model's weights. You save one whenever the validation loss improves, so the best model is never lost.
  • Early stopping: if the validation loss has not improved for patience epochs in a row, stop. Why wait several epochs? Validation loss is noisy, and one bad epoch does not mean overfitting has started.
  • Restore the best: after the loop, load the best checkpoint back. Otherwise you would keep the last (often worse) weights.
  • History: a dictionary of lists, one value per epoch, so you can plot and compare runs.
  • log_fn: a function you can pass in. fit calls it each epoch, and that is how an experiment tracker plugs in without changing the loop.
A detail that trips people up: average the loss over examples, not over batches. The last batch is often smaller, so multiply each batch's mean loss by its size and divide by the total number of examples at the end.

5. Fighting overfitting

  • Dropout: during training, randomly set a fraction of unit outputs to 0 (for example 30% to 50%). The network cannot rely on any single unit. At evaluation time dropout is turned off, which is why model.eval() matters.
  • Weight decay: a small penalty that nudges weights toward zero. Smaller weights give smoother, less memorizing models. Use it with AdamW, typically a value around 0.01 to 0.1.
  • Early stopping: stop when validation stops improving, as in fit().
  • More data or augmentation: the strongest cure. Augmentation creates new training examples by flipping, cropping or shifting existing ones.

Optimizers and the learning rate

SGD takes a step against the gradient. Adam adapts the step for each weight and usually works well with little tuning. AdamW is Adam with a cleaner version of weight decay, and it is a good default. The learning rate is usually the most important setting: too small learns too slowly, too large is unstable.

A scheduler lowers the learning rate during training, for example smoothly to near zero (cosine annealing). Big steps early, careful steps late.

6. Tracking experiments and running sweeps

After a few runs you forget which settings gave which result. An experiment tracker (Weights & Biases, MLflow) records, for every run: the settings, the metrics each epoch, and the final result. You then compare runs side by side.

  • Log: the config, the metrics each epoch, the seed, and a note about what you changed.
  • Seeds: fix the random seed so a run can be repeated. Differences between runs should come from your settings, not from luck.
  • One change at a time: if you change three things and the score improves, you do not know which one helped.

A sweep runs many settings automatically. You will run a grid: 5 learning rates times 2 batch sizes.

Is the winner really better? With 2,000 validation images, an accuracy of 84% is only known to about plus or minus 0.8 percentage points (one standard error is roughly sqrt(0.84 x 0.16 / 2000) = 0.8%). So two runs that differ by half a point are probably tied. Prefer a setting that is good across neighbouring values over one lucky peak.

Finally, remember the exam rule: after choosing the best setting using validation, score it on the test set once. Report that number.

8. Common pitfalls

  • Tuning on the test set, so the final number is no longer honest.
  • Averaging the loss over batches instead of over examples.
  • Forgetting model.eval() and torch.no_grad() when validating.
  • Forgetting model.train(), so dropout silently stays off.
  • Saving the last model instead of the best one.
  • Changing several settings at once, then guessing what helped.
  • Believing a tiny difference between runs when the validation set is small.

9. Check yourself

Try each question first, then open the answer.

Question 1: Training accuracy is 98%, validation 84%, and test 83%. What is happening, and what is your first move?

A 14-point gap between training and validation means overfitting. Try dropout, weight decay, early stopping, or more data. The test score being close to validation is a good sign that your choices were honest.

Question 2: You tried 50 settings and report the best validation accuracy, 86%, as your final result. Why is that too optimistic?

You picked the winner because it scored highest on that validation set, so part of the score is luck. Score the chosen model once on the test set and report that instead.

Question 3: Early stopping with patience 5 stopped at epoch 14. Which epoch's weights should you keep, and why?

The best epoch, which is epoch 9 here (five epochs earlier), where the validation loss was lowest. That is what the saved checkpoint holds, which is why fit() loads it back at the end.
