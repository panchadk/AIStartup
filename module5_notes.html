1. The big picture

Almost every vision product, from a plant-disease checker to a quality-control camera, follows the same recipe today: start from a pretrained model, replace its last layer, and adapt it with your own photos. Nobody trains a vision model from nothing unless they have millions of images.

That shifts where the real work is. The model is a commodity that you download for free. Your photos, your labels, and your honest evaluation are the product. This module is about doing those well.

2. How a network sees an image

A convolutional neural network (CNN) slides small filters across the image. Each filter detects a simple pattern wherever it appears. Stacking many layers of filters builds up understanding:

  • Early layers detect edges and colour blobs. Every photo contains them, so these are useful for any project.
  • Middle layers combine edges into textures and corners.
  • Late layers detect parts such as wheels, ears or leaves.
  • The head (the final layer) turns those features into scores for your classes.

This is why a model trained on pictures of cars and dogs can help you spot plant diseases: the early and middle layers are already good at seeing.

3. Transfer learning

A model such as ResNet-18 has been trained on ImageNet, 1.2 million photos in 1,000 classes. Everything it learned is stored in its weights. In transfer learning you keep those weights (the backbone) and replace only the final layer (the head) with a new one for your classes.

Look at the sizes. ResNet-18 has about 11.2 million weights. For a 3-class problem the new head has 512 x 3 + 3 = 1,539 weights. Learning 1,539 numbers from 150 photos is realistic. Learning 11 million is not, and that is why the scratch baseline in the notebook struggles.

The recipe in code: load the pretrained model, replace model.fc with nn.Linear(512, number_of_classes). That is all the "new head" is.

4. Freezing and fine-tuning

You choose how much of the backbone is allowed to change. A parameter that is frozen keeps its pretrained value (requires_grad = False).

  1. Feature extraction: freeze the whole backbone and train only the head. Fast, safe, and hard to overfit. Use a normal learning rate such as 0.001.
  2. Fine-tuning: unfreeze the backbone and keep training, with a much lower learning rate such as 0.0001. Big steps would overwrite the useful pretrained features. Small steps gently adapt them to your photos.
Two details that cause bugs: (1) Freeze first, then add the new head, because a layer created after freezing is trainable by default. (2) After unfreezing, create a new optimizer, because the old one only knows the head's parameters.

5. Your data is the product

Collecting photos

  • At least 50 images per class, more if you can. Aim for the real conditions your product will meet.
  • Vary everything that should not matter: background, lighting, angle, distance, camera. If every photo of class A is on a white table, the model may learn the table instead of the object. This is called a shortcut.
  • Clean labels. A wrong label teaches the model wrong things and makes your test score unreliable.
  • No duplicates. Copies and near-copies spread across the train and test sets give inflated scores, as you saw in Module 4.

Splitting

Use a stratified split: divide each class separately into train, validation and test. With small data, a plain random split can leave a class almost out of the test set by bad luck.

Preprocessing and augmentation

Pretrained models expect a certain input: a fixed image size (224 pixels for most) and pixel values normalized with the ImageNet mean and standard deviation. Skipping this is a common cause of poor results.

Augmentation randomly changes training photos a little each epoch: flipping, cropping and zooming, small colour changes. The model sees a slightly different version each time, so it cannot memorize exact pixels. Never augment validation or test images, because you want repeatable scores on real photos. Only use changes that keep the label true: flipping a photo of a cat is fine, flipping the digit 6 is not.

6. Evaluating a vision model

Accuracy is one number. A confusion matrix shows where the mistakes are: rows are the true classes, columns are the predicted classes, and the diagonal is correct.

  • Per-class recall is the diagonal cell divided by the row total: of all true dogs, how many were found?
  • Look at the worst mistakes: the wrong predictions the model was most confident about. They often reveal bad labels, misleading backgrounds, or genuinely ambiguous classes. Fixing the data usually beats tuning the model.
Small test sets are noisy. If 22 of 24 test images are correct (91.7%), the standard error is about sqrt(0.917 x 0.083 / 24) = 5.6 points. So the true accuracy could easily be anywhere from about 86% to 97%. With only 24 images you cannot tell a 91% model from an 88% model. Collect more test photos before drawing strong conclusions.

7. How much data do you need?

The gap between pretrained and scratch is largest when data is scarce, which is exactly the situation of most startups. As you collect more data the gap narrows, but pretrained models usually stay ahead. The practical advice: start with a pretrained model, collect 50 images per class, look at the mistakes, and let them tell you which photos to collect next.

9. Common pitfalls

  • Skipping Normalize, or using the wrong statistics, so a pretrained model sees unfamiliar inputs.
  • Augmenting the validation or test images.
  • Freezing after replacing the head, so nothing trains.
  • Forgetting to build a new optimizer after unfreezing.
  • Using a high learning rate when fine-tuning, which destroys the pretrained features.
  • Collecting photos with a hidden shortcut, such as one class always on the same background.
  • Trusting a small test set too much, or tuning on it.

10. Check yourself

Try each question first, then open the answer.

Question 1: ResNet-18 has about 11.2 million parameters. You freeze the backbone and add a new head for 4 classes. How many parameters train?

The head is one Linear layer from 512 features to 4 classes: 512 x 4 + 4 = 2,052 parameters. That is under 0.02% of the model.

Question 2: Why are the validation and test images not augmented?

Because you want repeatable scores that measure how the model does on real, unmodified photos. Random changes at evaluation time would make the numbers jump around and would not reflect what your product will see.

Question 3: Your test set has 24 images and the model gets 22 right. Can you be sure the true accuracy is above 85%?

No. The accuracy is 91.7%, but the standard error is about 5.6 points, so the true accuracy could plausibly be around 86% to 97%, and 85% is not safely excluded. Collect more test photos for a firmer answer.
