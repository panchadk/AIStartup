1. The big picture

Teams that build AI products learn the same lesson again and again: the data and the test matter more than the model. A better architecture rarely rescues messy labels or a misleading evaluation. A good dataset and an honest test make every model decision easier, and they stay valuable when you change models.

2. Collecting data

  • Look like the real thing. Collect examples from where your product will actually be used, not from the most convenient place. A model trained on tidy examples fails on real, messy ones.
  • Cover the variety: short and long, polite and angry, typos, different channels, different customer types.
  • Include the rare cases. If the important class is 5% of the world, you still need enough examples of it to learn from and to test on. Sometimes you collect more of the rare class on purpose, and remember that this changes the proportions.
  • Look at the data. Read a few hundred examples yourself. You will find duplicates, empty rows, strange items and labels you disagree with.
  • Respect privacy. Remove or avoid personal data you do not need.

3. Labeling and the labeling guide

A label is a decision, and decisions need rules. Without written rules, two people (or the same person on two days) will label the same item differently, and the model will learn the noise.

  • Define every label in terms of the situation, not keywords. "Urgent" means a customer who cannot work, not a message that contains the word "urgent".
  • Give examples of each label, including some that are not obvious.
  • Write down the edge cases as you find them, with the rule that decides them.
  • Have a tie-break: who decides when labelers disagree, and how the guide is updated afterwards.
  • Keep the guide in version control with the data. A label means nothing without the guide that defines it.

4. Do the labelers agree?

Ask a second person to label the same 50 to 100 items. If they often disagree with you, the guide is unclear, and no model can be reliably better than the labels it learns from and is tested on.

Raw agreement flatters. If 95% of items are normal, two people who always say "normal" agree 95% of the time without doing anything. Cohen's kappa corrects for agreement by chance:

kappa = (observed agreement - chance agreement) / (1 - chance agreement)

Example: two labelers, labels y/n.  A says y, y, n, n.  B says y, n, n, n.
  observed agreement = 3 of 4 = 0.75
  chance agreement   = (0.5 x 0.25) + (0.5 x 0.75) = 0.5
  kappa              = (0.75 - 0.5) / (1 - 0.5) = 0.5

A rough guide: above 0.8 is very good, 0.6 to 0.8 is good, and below 0.4 means that the guide needs work. Read the disagreements: they show you exactly which rule is missing.

5. The fixed test set

You split the data into three parts, as in Module 3: train (the model learns from it), validation (you make decisions with it), and test (the final exam). Two extra rules for a product team:

  • Stratify the split so that every part has the same proportion of each label. With a rare class, a random split could leave the test set with almost none of it.
  • Freeze the test set. Never train on it, never tune on it, never fix labels in it quietly. Then scores from last month and today are comparable. A fingerprint (a short code computed from the content) lets you prove it has not changed: the same fingerprint in two reports means the same test set.

Clean before you split: remove empty items and exact duplicates, or a copy of a training example in the test set will flatter your score.

6. Metrics that tell the truth

Suppose 12% of your tickets are urgent. A "model" that always answers normal is 88% accurate and completely useless. Accuracy rewards doing nothing when one class dominates.

To see what really happens, count the four kinds of outcome for the class you care about (the positive class):

  • Precision: when the model says urgent, how often is it right? It falls when there are many false alarms.
  • Recall: of all the really urgent tickets, how many did it find? It falls when urgent tickets are missed.
  • F1 combines the two (their harmonic mean), so a model cannot hide a bad one behind a good one.
Choose the metric by the cost of the mistake. Missing an urgent ticket, a fraud case or a defective part is expensive: watch recall. Blocking a legitimate customer or interrupting a person is expensive: watch precision. This is a business question first and a modelling question second. Many products set a minimum for one and optimize the other.

Always show the majority baseline next to the accuracy. A number means little until you know what doing nothing would score.

7. The evaluation harness

An evaluation harness is the code that runs a model on the test set and produces a report, the same way every time, with no manual steps. In this module it is one function, run_eval(predict_fn, test_set, positive).

  • It takes a predict function, so any model (a classifier, a prompt, a fine-tuned model) can be plugged in and compared fairly.
  • It returns a report: the metrics, the baseline, the slices, the list of failures, and the test set fingerprint.
  • It is glue code, written once from small, tested pieces. When you change models, you do not rewrite it.
  • Save the reports with a date and the fingerprint. They are your history: how the product got better, and proof for a customer or an investor.

8. Slices and bias

An average can hide unequal treatment. A slice is a group of inputs that you evaluate separately: a language, a region, a device, a channel, a customer type, short versus long text.

Gaps arise for ordinary reasons: a group has fewer training examples, its inputs look different (short, informal, another dialect), or its labels were noisier. They matter because real users sit in those groups. Check the slices that matter for your product, and ask who is hurt when the model fails. Then fix it with more and better data for that group, better features, or a separate threshold, and measure again.

9. Failure analysis

The metrics say how much the model fails. Failure analysis says why:

  1. Collect the failures (on the validation set, so that the test set stays untouched).
  2. Read them. Look for patterns in the input: very short, negation, a topic, a style, a rare word, a wrong label.
  3. Write a rule for each pattern and count the failures per category.
  4. Fix the largest category first.

Fixes come in two families. Data fixes (more examples of the failing pattern, cleaner labels, better coverage) are usually cheaper and more durable than model fixes (class weights, a different model, extra features). After any fix, measure on the same test set and report the change in precision and recall, not just accuracy. An honest "it did not help" is a useful result: it tells you where not to spend next week.

11. Common pitfalls

  • Reporting accuracy without the majority baseline.
  • Labels with no written guide, so that nobody agrees with anybody.
  • Duplicates or near-duplicates across the train and test sets.
  • Tuning on the test set, or changing it between reports.
  • Looking only at averages and never at slices.
  • Analyzing failures without counting them, then fixing the loudest one rather than the biggest.
  • Judging a fix by accuracy alone.

12. Check yourself

Try each question first, then open the answer.

Question 1: A dataset has 1,000 items and only 20 are positive. A model predicts negative for every item. What are its accuracy and its recall?

Accuracy is 980 / 1,000 = 98%. Recall is 0 / 20 = 0%, because it finds none of the positives. This is why you should never trust accuracy alone on imbalanced data.

Question 2: A model finds 45 of the 60 really urgent tickets and also wrongly flags 15 normal tickets as urgent. What are its precision, recall and F1?

TP = 45, FN = 15, FP = 15. Precision = 45 / (45 + 15) = 0.75. Recall = 45 / (45 + 15) = 0.75. F1 = 2 x 0.75 x 0.75 / 1.5 = 0.75.

Question 3: Two labelers agree on 90% of the items, and chance agreement is 50%. What is Cohen's kappa, and what does it tell you?

kappa = (0.90 - 0.50) / (1 - 0.50) = 0.80. That is very good agreement: the guide is clear enough, and the labels are a solid basis for training and testing.
