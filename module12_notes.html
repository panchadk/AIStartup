1. The big picture

In Module 11 you asked: does it work? Now there are three new questions: what happens when it does not? (hardening), does it pay? (economics), and can a stranger understand and believe it in five minutes? (the report and the pitch).

These are the things that separate a demo from a product, and a student project from a startup. None of them needs a better model.

2. Hardening

A prototype works when you feed it good input. A product meets input from the real world: empty, enormous, the wrong type, or deliberately hostile. Hardening is a few layers of protection:

  • Validate the input before the model sees it: type, emptiness, length, and for images the file type and size. Rejecting a bad request is free; running the model on it is not.
  • Contain crashes. Wrap the model call in try / except Exception, so that one bad input cannot take the service down.
  • Do not leak internals. The user gets a generic message ("the model could not process this input"). The exact exception, which may contain file paths, library names or secrets, goes to your log.
  • Log safely (Module 10): lengths and hashes, not private text.
  • Watch it: a health check, latency, error rate. You cannot fix what you cannot see.
Attack your own project. Send it None, an empty string, 5,000 characters, a number, a list, strange symbols, and an input that you know crashes the model. Whatever breaks in your hands will break in a stranger's.

3. Does the business add up?

Four numbers describe the economics of a service:

revenue        = price per request x requests
variable cost  = cost per request x requests
gross profit   = revenue - variable cost
gross margin   = gross profit / revenue
net profit     = gross profit - fixed monthly costs
break-even     = fixed monthly costs / (price - cost per request)   [requests per month]

A worked example (the demo project): price 1 cent, compute 0.005 cents, fixed costs 150 dollars a month, 100,000 requests a month. Revenue is 1,000 dollars, compute 5 dollars, gross margin 99.5%, net profit 845 dollars, and break-even at about 15,000 requests a month.

Price from value, not from cost

The compute for an AI request is often a tiny fraction of a cent. If you charged cost plus a margin, you would earn too little to pay a single month of fixed costs. The right question is: what is the problem worth to the customer? What do they pay today (a person's time, lost sales, a competitor)? Price somewhere well below that value, and well above your cost.

Three scenarios

Never present one number. Build a pessimistic, a base and an optimistic case (different price and volume). The pessimistic case tells you whether you survive, and the number an investor questions first is usually the assumption you are least sure of. Say which it is, and how you would test it with pilot customers.

4. The technical report and honest limitations

The report lets someone else understand, trust and continue your work. It has six sections: summary, data, model choice, evaluation, limitations, next steps. It is not a sales document, and its credibility comes from its honesty.

  • Failure cases: where it goes wrong, with examples and rates.
  • Data gaps: what the data does not cover (languages, regions, kinds of customers), and how it was made (generated, templated, collected).
  • Risks: unequal performance across groups, privacy, misuse, the cost of a wrong answer.

Saying your limitations yourself, first, is not a weakness. It tells the reader that you looked, and it is the quickest way to earn belief in the rest.

5. The five-minute pitch

  1. Problem (40 s): one user, one pain, one number.
  2. Demo (90 s): show it working live, before explaining it. Include one case where it handles something tricky.
  3. How it works (50 s): one simple picture.
  4. Results (50 s): your numbers against the baseline, and one honest limitation.
  5. Cost and market (40 s): what it costs, what you charge, who pays.
  6. Next steps (30 s): what you need and what you will do first.
  • Rehearse out loud with a timer at least three times. The seconds in your plan must be the seconds you really take.
  • Prepare for failure: a backup screen recording, and the demo page already open and awake.
  • Nobody remembers the fifth thing you said. Make the first two count.

6. Defensibility

An investor, a customer or a competitor will ask: what stops someone else doing this? If your answer is "our model", it is a weak answer, because the same pretrained models are available to everyone.

  • Proprietary data, especially labeled data that grows with every customer.
  • Workflow integration: being part of the tools your customers use every day makes you costly to leave.
  • Distribution: reaching the customers first, cheaply, with trust.
  • Speed of learning: the loop of Module 9 (evaluate, find failures, fix) run faster than the competition.

7. The README and open source

The README is the front door of your repository. A stranger should understand what it is, try it in minutes, and see what it can and cannot do. It needs: a title and two sentences on what it does; a link to the live demo; numbered steps to run it (with the install command); your results with the baseline; and your limitations.

  • Test it the way a user would: ask someone to follow it on a clean machine, without your help.
  • If you publish the repository, add a license, pin library versions, and make sure that no key or private data is in the files or in the history: a secret in an old commit is still public.

8. Demo day

  • Open your demo page a minute before, so that a sleeping Space is awake.
  • Know which input you will paste, and test it that day.
  • Have the numbers for the questions you will be asked: baseline, failures, cost, who pays, and what stops a copycat.
  • When giving feedback to others: one thing that worked, one question, one suggestion. Specific beats nice, and kind beats clever.

9. Key terms

TermPlain meaning
HardeningMaking a service survive bad input, crashes and misuse
Unit economicsThe money earned and spent for each unit of use, such as one request
Gross marginThe share of revenue left after the cost of serving
Break-evenThe volume at which total revenue equals total cost
Fixed costsCosts you pay however much the service is used
LimitationsAn honest account of failures, data gaps and risks
DefensibilityWhat makes your product hard to copy
READMEThe document that tells a stranger what the project is and how to run it

10. Common pitfalls

  • Putting internal error details in what the user sees.
  • Pricing from your cost instead of from the customer's value.
  • One optimistic number instead of three scenarios.
  • Vague limitations, or none.
  • Starting the pitch with the model instead of the problem.
  • A live demo that was not rehearsed, with no backup.
  • Saying "deployed" when no stranger has used it.

11. Check yourself

Try each question first, then open the answer.

Question 1: A product charges 0.02 dollars per request, costs 0.004 dollars per request to serve, and has 80 dollars of fixed costs a month. How many requests a month does it need to break even?

Profit per request = 0.02 - 0.004 = 0.016 dollars. Break-even = 80 / 0.016 = 5,000 requests a month. Below that it loses money; above it, each extra request adds 1.6 cents.

Question 2: Your pitch plan adds up to 330 seconds. What do you cut, and what do you keep?

Keep the demo: it is the part that convinces. Cut from the explanation and the detail: shorten how it works to a single picture, trim cost and market to two sentences, and drop any point that does not help the audience decide. You need to remove at least 30 seconds, then rehearse with a timer to make sure the real time matches the plan.

Question 3: A teammate wants to leave the limitations out of the report because they make the project look weak. What do you say?

Hiding limitations is the weaker move. Readers, customers and investors find failures anyway, and when they find ones you did not mention, they stop believing everything else. Specific limitations (with numbers) show that you evaluated the work seriously, tell the reader how far to trust it, and give you your plan for what to improve next.
