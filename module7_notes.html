1. The big picture

In Module 6 you built a search engine: give it a question and it returns the passages most likely to contain the answer. A RAG chatbot adds one more step. It hands those passages to a language model and asks it to write the answer, using only what the passages say.

Think of it as an open-book exam. The model is the student, your documents are the book, and your search engine is the student's index. A good student looks things up and quotes the page. A bad one answers from memory and hopes.

2. Why RAG?

A language model on its own has three problems for a business product:

  • It has never seen your documents. Your policies, your manuals, your customers' data are not in its training.
  • Its knowledge is frozen. It knows nothing that happened after it was trained.
  • It can hallucinate. When it does not know, it often produces a fluent, confident, false answer, and it cannot tell you where the answer came from.

RAG fixes all three without retraining the model. To change what the chatbot knows, you change the documents, not the model. That is why it is the most common pattern in AI products today.

3. The prompt is the contract

The model does exactly what its prompt asks, so the prompt must spell out the rules. It has two parts: a system message with the rules, and a user message with the evidence and the question.

A good RAG system message says four things:

  1. Use only the passages. The word "only" matters. Without it, the model mixes in its own knowledge.
  2. Cite by number in square brackets.
  3. If the answer is not in the passages, say a fixed sentence. Giving the exact words lets your code detect an abstention reliably.
  4. Keep it short. Shorter answers cost less and are easier to check.

The order inside the user message also matters: evidence first, question last. Many models pay most attention to the end of the prompt.

4. Citations

A citation connects a claim to its source. In this module the model writes passage numbers like [2], and your code turns each number into the real source: the document name and the passage text.

  • Users can verify. A person can open the source and check the answer. That is what makes a chatbot trustworthy.
  • You can debug. If a bad answer cites an unrelated passage, you know the problem is in generation. If it cites nothing useful, look at retrieval.
  • Parse defensively. Models write [2], [1, 3], and sometimes numbers that do not exist. Accept lists, and ignore numbers out of range.
  • A citation is a claim, not proof. The model can cite a passage that does not actually support its answer. That is why the grading in this module checks that the cited passage contains the right information.

5. Controlling hallucination

You cannot make a model incapable of hallucinating, but you can make it much less likely. Use three layers:

  1. The prompt: "only the passages" and a fixed "I don't know" sentence.
  2. The gate: before calling the model at all, look at the best retrieval score. If even the best passage is not similar to the question, the documents probably do not cover it. Skip the model and answer "I don't know" directly. This is both safer and cheaper.
  3. Checking: verify that answers cite real passages, and test with questions the documents cannot answer.

Choosing the line is a trade-off. Set it too high and you block questions that the documents could answer. Set it too low and unanswerable questions slip through to the model. Look at your own scores, and pick a line that blocks almost no answerable question.

6. Where RAG goes wrong

When an answer is wrong, ask the questions in this order:

  1. Was the right passage in the retrieved set? If not, it is a retrieval problem: larger k, better chunks, or the information is not in your documents.
  2. If it was there, did the model use it? If not, strengthen the prompt, or use a stronger model.
  3. Is the citation present and correct?
  4. For an unanswerable question: did it abstain? If not, raise the gate and tighten the abstain rule.

This order is the same debugging discipline as Module 4: start with the data (here, the retrieved evidence), and only then blame the model.

7. Testing a RAG chatbot

Write a test set with two kinds of questions:

  • Answerable (for example 15): each with a gold phrase that marks the right passage. A correct response answers and cites a passage containing the gold phrase.
  • Unanswerable (for example 5): plausible questions the documents do not cover. A correct response is "I don't know".

The unanswerable questions are not a detail. A chatbot that always answers will score well on the answerable questions and fail the one thing users care about: not being misled.

The automatic score is a proxy. It checks the sources, not the wording. An answer can cite the right passage and still say something slightly wrong. So also read a sample of the answers yourself and judge whether the cited passage really supports what was said. Automatic scores find the problems; reading the answers explains them.

8. What an answer costs

Language models are priced by tokens (pieces of words), usually per million. You pay for the tokens you send in and the tokens that come out, at different prices.

A worked example with hypothetical prices of 1 dollar per million input tokens and 5 dollars per million output tokens (always check your provider's real, current prices):

k = 4:  450 tokens in, 40 tokens out
        450/1,000,000 x 1  +  40/1,000,000 x 5  =  0.00045 + 0.00020  =  $0.00065 per question
        10,000 questions  ->  $6.50

k = 2:  270 tokens in, 40 tokens out
        0.00027 + 0.00020  =  $0.00047 per question
        10,000 questions  ->  $4.70

Ways to reduce the cost: fewer or shorter passages, the score gate (no call at all for questions you cannot answer), a smaller or cheaper model, and a shorter answer limit. But measure what each change does to accuracy, because cheap and wrong is not a saving.

Speed and reliabilityDepends on your hardwareFast and consistent

A startup usually prototypes with whatever is cheapest to try, measures with a test set like yours, and then chooses the model that meets its quality target at an acceptable cost. That is exactly the comparison in the stretch challenge, and it leads to the next module.

Keep your keys secret. An API key is like a password that spends your money. Store it in Colab's Secrets panel, never in the notebook text, and never commit it to GitHub.

10. Key terms

AbstainSay "I don't know" instead of guessing
Gate (min_score)A retrieval-score limit below which the model is not called

11. Common pitfalls

  • A prompt that does not say "only", so the model mixes in outside knowledge.
  • No fixed abstain sentence, so abstentions cannot be detected.
  • Parsing only [n] and missing [n, m], or trusting numbers that do not exist.
  • Tuning the prompt when the real problem is that retrieval missed.
  • Testing only questions the documents can answer.
  • Embedding questions with a different model than the documents.
  • Leaking an API key into a notebook or a repository.

12. Check yourself

Try each question first, then open the answer.

Question 1: Retrieval returned the right passage, but the chatbot's answer is wrong. Where is the problem?

In generation: the model ignored or misread the evidence. Strengthen the prompt (only the passages, cite them), or use a stronger model. Changing the chunking or the search would not help, because retrieval already did its job.

Question 2: The chatbot gives a confident answer, with a citation, to a question the documents cannot answer. Name two defences.

(1) The score gate: if the best retrieval score is below min_score, do not call the model and abstain. (2) A stricter prompt with a fixed "I don't know" sentence. You can also check whether the cited passage really supports the answer, and add more unanswerable questions to your tests.

Question 3: A prompt has 70 tokens of rules, 4 passages of 90 tokens each, a 20-token question, and the answer has 40 tokens. With hypothetical prices of 1 and 5 dollars per million input and output tokens, what does one answer cost, and what do 10,000 cost?

Input = 70 + 360 + 20 = 450 tokens. Cost = 450/1,000,000 x 1 + 40/1,000,000 x 5 = 0.00045 + 0.0002 = $0.00065. For 10,000 questions that is $6.50.
